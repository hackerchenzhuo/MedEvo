MedEvo turns failed medical reasoning traces into supervision. An answer-blind auditor guides a local repair; independent feedback directs which failures to revisit in the next round.

The research question

What is worth keeping
from a failed answer?

A student may read an image correctly and make several supported inferences before one mistake changes its conclusion. MedEvo asks how to preserve those useful steps, repair the failed operation, and turn the result into supervision the student can learn from.

The next decision is just as important: which failures deserve more training effort?

01 / Method

Knowledge Navigation connects
local repair to the next learning round.

Medical tags describe the clinical context. Error types describe the reasoning operation that failed. MedEvo uses them at two different decision points.

A

Within a trajectory

Choose where and how to repair.

  1. Locate the error.Step uncertainty suggests where to inspect. The auditor checks the evidence and identifies a safe boundary.
  2. Give targeted guidance.The auditor uses observable medical tags and an error diagnosis to write a minimal hint without the reference answer.
  3. Continue and screen.The same student preserves the prefix and regenerates the suffix. Correctness, prefix integrity, unsupported additions, and learnability are checked before admission.

Output → screened student-generated supervision

B

Across learning rounds

Choose what to learn next.

  1. Measure remaining weaknesses.A fixed feedback set identifies errors and regressions across medical tags and error types.
  2. Select questions for fresh rollouts.These gaps prioritize questions in the training pool and the types of failures to repair.
  3. Update the supervision.Keep at most one active target per question. Train the next student from the original base model on the updated dataset.

Output → revised data priorities and training targets

What changes between rounds?

The taxonomy and error codebook stay fixed. Data priorities and accepted training targets evolve. Experience passes through the dataset: each round of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) starts again from the same base model.

02 / What the experiments show

Judge a repair by what
the student learns from it.

Repair success measures whether a failed response can be corrected. Downstream accuracy measures how useful the resulting supervision is. The experiments expose why both matter.

Finding 01

Harder repairs can produce more useful supervision.

Selecting the 8,000 questions with the longest failed traces yields lower repair success but higher downstream accuracy than selecting 8,000 random questions.

The two metrics rank these selections in opposite orders. This supports checking learning outcomes when choosing what to repair. It does not isolate trace length from question difficulty.

Finding 02

Preserve the prefix and diagnose the failed operation.

Type-guided suffix continuation performs best among the four first-round designs: 71.80% repair success and 67.22% mean accuracy. Both the regeneration scope and the specificity of the hint affect the result.

Finding 03

Better fit to training failures can hide worse generalization.

Training-error feedback raises training accuracy to 78.00%, while benchmark accuracy falls to 64.01%. Fixed-set feedback reaches 69.12% on the benchmarks. The source of feedback changes what the next round learns.

Practical implication

Use training failures to find repair candidates, assess remaining capability gaps on independent data, and check the effect of the resulting supervision on downstream learning.

Where these findings apply

The study covers medical image question answering in multiple-choice and short-answer formats. The current system uses a fixed taxonomy and error codebook, and relies on a strong proprietary auditor. The trajectory-selection study does not separate length from difficulty. Clinical use and transfer to other settings require further evaluation.
